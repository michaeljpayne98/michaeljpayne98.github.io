Module 3 · Case study 1 of 4

Anchor's Edge

A monthly professional development series at Vanderbilt with three session types: Manager Monday, Take Off Tuesday and Thrive Thursday.

  • 80+ NPS
  • About 60 live per session
  • 30+ sessions (approx.)

1 My Role

Anchor's Edge was already running when I joined the team supporting it in January 2026. I went on to run it, and from March 2026 I recruited every speaker for all three session types.

My focus was continuity: three session types every month, each with its own speaker, and no drop in quality through the handoff.

2 The Approach

  • Plan months ahead. I planned sessions a few months out, so speakers had time to prepare.
  • Coach every presenter. Before each session, I offered consulting to help presenters generate ideas, practice and build their content.
  • Set them up for the day. Each speaker got a facilitator brief and a run-of-show document.
  • Extend the reach. I turned sessions into on-demand e-learning modules, built in Articulate with Camtasia, so people who missed the live session could still learn.

3 The Results

  • 80+ NPS from 100+ survey responses
  • About 60 live attendees per session 80 to 90 registered
  • 88 / 125 record session live / registered
  • 30+ sessions approx. · since January 2026
  • 20+ e-learning modules approx. · built from sessions, mostly on professional development and AI

Vanderbilt Voyage

Vanderbilt University's new employee experience. I facilitated it; I didn't design it.

  • 1,200+ new hires (approx.)
  • 75 to 80 NPS
  • 40+ sessions (approx.)

1 The Challenge

Every Voyage room was a mix. People from security, dining and exempt roles sat side by side, with different backgrounds, education and jobs. Sometimes there was a language barrier too. One session had to work for all of them.

2 My Role

I facilitated Vanderbilt Voyage from February 2025 to November 2026, leading 2 to 3 sessions a month for 30 to 50 new hires each.

3 The Approach

  • Remove barriers first. I made sure every learner had what they needed to succeed, including a translator when there was a language barrier.
  • Make it welcoming. I created a safe space where people felt comfortable taking part, whatever their role or background.
  • Open with three easy questions. Everyone met three people and asked them:
    1. What's your go-to morning drink?
    2. What's your favorite restaurant in Nashville?
    3. Does pineapple belong on pizza?
    Then we came back together and introduced a few people. Anyone new to the city left with food recommendations, and everyone got to know each other a little.

4 The Results

  • 1,200+ new hires welcomed approx. · February 2025 to November 2026
  • 40+ sessions facilitated approx. · 2 to 3 a month
  • 75 to 80 NPS about 75% of attendees respond
  • 30 to 50 new hires per session

Module 3 · Case study 3 of 4

Dore to Dore Navigator Program

A program that trains and certifies Vanderbilt staff as volunteer facilitators. I led its rebuild from the ground up.

  • Status: in design (November 2026)
  • 20 Navigators re-engaged
  • 5-part blended pathway

Certified Navigators deliver Vanderbilt Voyage and other People, Culture and Belonging programming. They can also guest speak at Voyage, present at Anchor's Edge, join monthly lunch-and-learns, and observe or co-facilitate sessions to stay sharp. Navigators gain facilitation and leadership experience and a campus-wide network. Vanderbilt gets a steady pool of trained facilitators.

1 The Challenge

  • No outside certification. The original model relied on an external ATD certification that was no longer an option.
  • Navigators had drifted. Many people from Cohorts 1 and 2 had stepped back.
  • Nothing kept them involved. There was no structured way to keep Navigators engaged after certification, so drop-off from busy workloads was a real risk.

2 My Role

I owned the rebuild on my own, from July 2026 to November 2026. I wrote the program plan, the standard operating procedure (the step-by-step guide for running it) and a handoff guide, and I led the outreach to bring past Navigators back.

3 The Approach

Why blended

  • It fits the budget. The new model uses tools Vanderbilt already has (Rise, Teams and PowerPoint), at almost no cost beyond facilitator time.
  • It respects busy schedules. Navigators have full-time jobs. Self-paced content fits around their work, and in-person time is saved for what needs a room: practice, feedback and community.

The new pathway

  1. Orientation and Foundations In person · 2.5 hours Why the program exists, roles alongside the operations team, how adults learn, planning a session and facilitation presence.
  2. Facilitation Skills In person · 2.5 hours Engagement and questioning, reading the room, inclusive facilitation, difficult moments, feedback and strong closings.
  3. Voyage Content Deep Dive Self-paced in Rise · about 30 minutes Each section of Voyage Day 1, with knowledge checks.
  4. Working session (optional) In person Open office hours to prepare the capstone.
  5. Capstone In person Small groups design and present a learning program from scratch. This earns certification.

Bringing Navigators back

On October 1, 2026, I sent a personal email to everyone from Cohorts 1 and 2 with a short interest survey called "We Want You Back." It was built to:

  • Invite them to help shape the program's next chapter, not just ask them to return.
  • Welcome everyone, whether they'd stayed active or stepped back.
  • Make coming back easy and low-pressure.

4 The Results

  • 20 past Navigators recommitted to staying active out of roughly 40 to 50 reached
  • 5 part blended pathway designed in person and self-paced

The program was still in design when I left Vanderbilt in November 2026. I wrote a handoff guide so the work could continue.

Module 4 · Step 1 of 4

Needs analysis

To show how I design, I built a short sample course from scratch: Using AI as a Thinking Partner at Work. This module walks through my process, one step at a time.

About this sample

The organization, learners and scenario are made up for this portfolio. Nothing here comes from any employer's internal content or data.

The request

Imagine a leader says, "Our staff need AI training." Before I build anything, I find out what's really going on and what people need to do differently.

The learners

  • Staff at a large organization in non-technical roles, like coordinators, administrative staff and front-line support.
  • Little or no hands-on experience with AI tools.
  • Busy, with little time for training.
  • A wide mix of comfort levels with technology.

Questions I'd ask

  • What should people do differently after this training?
  • Where are people already using AI, and where are they avoiding it?
  • What worries them? Accuracy, privacy, their jobs?
  • What does the organization's AI policy allow?
  • How will we know the training worked?

How I'd gather answers

  • A short survey to see how people use AI today and how confident they feel.
  • Small focus groups across different roles to hear the real barriers.
  • A policy review with whoever owns the AI policy, so the training matches the rules.
  • A few real tasks observed, to see where AI could save time.

What this sample assumes

For this sample, I assumed the needs analysis found that:

  • Most learners have tried an AI chat tool once or twice but don't use it for real work.
  • They type short, vague requests, get generic answers and give up.
  • Some trust AI answers too much. Others don't trust them at all.
  • Many aren't sure what information is safe to share.

Is training the answer?

Partly. Training builds skill and judgment, but people also need clear rules and a quick reminder on the job. So the sample pairs a short e-learning module with a one-page job aid, and points learners to their organization's AI policy.

Learning objectives

Objectives turn the needs into something I can design and measure. Each one names what learners will do, not just what they'll know.

After this 5-minute module, learners will be able to:

  1. Write a prompt for a work task that includes a role, context, task and format.
  2. Check an AI response for errors and made-up details before using it.
  3. Decide when information is not safe to share with an AI tool.

Design choices

  • Short. About 5 minutes, because these learners are busy.
  • Practice over reading. Three of the six screens ask learners to make a decision.
  • One realistic scenario carries through the whole module, so it feels like real work.
  • Accessible. Everything works with a keyboard and a screen reader, with no audio or video required.

Module 4 · Step 4 of 4

The finished build

Here's the finished sample. Try it the way a learner would.

Using AI as a Thinking Partner at Work

Screen 1 of 6

Welcome

AI won't do your job for you. Used well, it helps you think faster and get started sooner.

In about 5 minutes, you'll practice how to:

  • Ask AI for exactly what you need.
  • Check what it gives you.
  • Keep private information safe.
Designer's note: Learners see the payoff and the three skills right away, so they know why the next 5 minutes matter.

The prompt recipe

A good prompt has four ingredients:

Role
Who should the AI act as? "Act as an experienced event planner."
Context
What's the situation? "Our 40-person team volunteered at a food bank on Friday."
Task
What do you need? "Draft a thank-you email to the team."
Format
What should it look like? "Under 150 words, warm tone, three bullet highlights."

Then keep the conversation going. Ask it to shorten the draft, change the tone or give you three options. That's what makes it a thinking partner.

Designer's note: One memorable model is easier to use on the job than a long list of tips.

Pick the better prompt

Jordan coordinates events for a 40-person team. After a volunteer day at a local food bank, Jordan needs to send the team a thank-you email.

Which prompt will get Jordan the most useful first draft?

Choose an answer first.

Not quite. There's no context, so the AI has to guess who the email is for and what happened. Expect something generic.

Closer. It names the task and the audience, but "make it good" doesn't say what good looks like. Add the details and a format.

Correct! It has all four ingredients: a role, context, a task and a format.

Designer's note: Every option gets its own feedback, so even a wrong answer teaches something.

Spot the problems

Here's the draft the AI wrote. Jordan only told it about the team, the food bank and the 600 meal kits.

Before Jordan sends it, which lines need checking? Select all that apply.

Select at least one line first.

Line 1 is fine. It matches what Jordan shared.

Line 2 is fine. Jordan gave the AI that number.

Line 3 needs checking. The AI made up a big claim. It sounds confident, but nobody gave it that fact.

Line 4 needs checking. Jordan never mentioned an Events Committee. The AI filled the gap with a guess.

Line 5 needs checking. The AI invented a date. Check every name, number and date before you send.

Designer's note: The made-up details sound believable on purpose. That's what AI mistakes look like in real life.

Safe to share?

Jordan wants help with next year's volunteer day. Should Jordan paste each of these into an AI tool?

The food bank's public address and website

Share. This is already public.

A coworker's medical reason for missing the event

Don't share. Health information is private and isn't needed for the task.

A spreadsheet of volunteers' home addresses and phone numbers

Don't share. That's personal contact information. Ask the AI for a template instead and fill in the details yourself.

Sort all three items first.

Always follow your organization's AI policy. When in doubt, leave it out.

Designer's note: The third item shows a safe alternative, so learners leave with a "do this instead," not just a "don't."

Wrap-up

You practiced three habits. Keep this job aid handy.

Job aid: AI as a thinking partner

  1. Ask with the recipe. Role, context, task, format. Then keep the conversation going.
  2. Check before you use. Verify every fact, name, number and date.
  3. Protect private information. Follow your AI policy. When in doubt, leave it out.
Designer's note: The job aid supports learners after the training ends, which is where behavior change actually happens.
